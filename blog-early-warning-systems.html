Maternal Early Warning Systems Fail Quietly: Why Alerts Alone Don't Prevent Severe Morbidity

AS

Anish J. Shah, MD, FACOG

June 24, 2026 • 9 min read

More than 80% of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States are considered preventable by the state committees that review them. That statistic is usually cited as an indictment of access or of clinical judgment. Increasingly it is neither. It is an indictment of timing.

Nearly every American labor unit now runs some form of maternal early warning system. The vital sign thresholds are posted, the escalation pathway is written, and the EHR fires an alert when the criteria are met. And yet severe maternal morbidity has not fallen the way early warning system pilots suggested it would.

Why Threshold Alerts Underperform

They fire at the end of the deterioration, not the beginning

A single-parameter threshold, whether systolic pressure above a cutoff or heart rate above a cutoff, is by construction a lagging indicator. Physiologic compensation in a young, healthy pregnant patient is remarkably good, which means vital signs often stay inside the normal range until compensation fails. By the time the alert fires, the patient has already crossed into the event the system was meant to prevent.

The obstetric hemorrhage case is the clearest example. Tachycardia and hypotension are late findings. A patient can lose a great deal of blood while her documented vital signs remain reassuring.

The alert does not carry the trajectory

A blood pressure of 148/94 means something very different as the fourth consecutive rising reading than as an isolated value in an anxious patient. Threshold systems treat them identically, because a threshold has no memory. The clinically meaningful signal, the slope, is exactly the part that gets discarded.

THE QUIET FAILURE
High sensitivity, low specificity, predictable outcome
Set thresholds tight enough to catch deterioration early and the alert fires on a large share of normal labors. Nurses on a busy unit learn within weeks which alerts are informative and which are noise. The system does not fail loudly. It fails by being silently discounted, which looks identical to working until the case that mattered gets discounted too.

Detection and response are treated as one problem

Recognition is necessary and insufficient. The deaths reviewed by maternal mortality review committees are full of cases where the abnormal value was documented, and sometimes even flagged, and the delay occurred afterward: in escalation, in obtaining blood products, in mobilizing the operating room, in reaching a physician who was in another delivery.

An early warning system that improves recognition in a unit whose response capacity is the actual bottleneck will produce very little change in outcomes. This is why the AIM patient safety bundles pair recognition elements with readiness elements: hemorrhage carts, massive transfusion protocols, unit-based drills. The bundle works as a bundle.

What Improves on a Threshold

Trajectory instead of cutoffs

Aggregate-weighted scoring and trend-based monitoring both outperform single-parameter thresholds, for the same reason: they use the sequence. Any approach that considers the direction and rate of change in vital signs, in the context of the patient's own baseline, detects deterioration meaningfully earlier than a fixed cutoff.

Risk stratification before the event window

Hemorrhage risk assessment on admission and reassessment in labor is now standard practice, and it works, but only when the resulting risk level actually changes something: where the patient is placed, whether a type and screen or crossmatch is ordered, whether quantitative blood loss measurement is set up before delivery rather than reconstructed after.

Quantitative blood loss, consistently

Visual estimation of blood loss is unreliable in a well-documented and consistent direction: it underestimates. Weight-based quantification is a low-technology intervention with an outsized effect on how early hemorrhage gets recognized as hemorrhage.

Escalation that names a person

The most effective escalation pathways specify who is called, in what order, with what response time expectation, and what happens when the first call is not answered. Pathways that specify a role rather than a mechanism tend to consume minutes at exactly the point where minutes are the scarce resource.

MEASURE THIS
Recognition-to-intervention interval
Most units track whether hemorrhage or severe hypertension occurred. Fewer track the elapsed time from first abnormal documented value to first therapeutic intervention. That interval is where preventability lives, it is measurable from data you already capture, and it is the number most likely to move when a system genuinely improves.

The Underlying Point

Early warning systems were designed to solve a recognition problem in an era of paper charts and intermittent vitals. That problem is largely solved. The data is captured continuously and it is already in the record. The remaining problem is interpretation: turning a stream of individually unremarkable values into an early statement about where this patient is heading.

That is a different kind of tool than a threshold alert, and it is the one the preventability statistic is asking for.
