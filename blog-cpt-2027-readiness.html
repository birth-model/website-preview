Coding & Reimbursement CPT 2027

The 90-Day CPT 2027 Countdown: An OB Revenue Cycle Readiness Checklist

AS

Anish J. Shah, MD, FACOG

September 15, 2026 • 9 min read

January 1, 2027 is now about a hundred days away. On that date, the CPT 2027 maternity care code set takes effect: the global obstetric package codes go away, labor management becomes separately reportable, and every delivery gets billed as a component service rather than a bundle. We have written in depth about what the new code set contains. This post is about something different: the operational work that has to happen before the code set arrives.

The practices that will struggle in January are not the ones that misunderstand the codes. They are the ones that understood the codes in October and still had not touched their charge master, their encounter forms, or their payer contracts by December.

Why a Coding Change Becomes a Cash-Flow Event

Global OB billing has one convenient property: a single charge, dropped once, after delivery. Everything upstream (the twelve or fourteen prenatal visits, the admission, the labor course) rolls into it. Your revenue cycle has been built around that shape for decades.

Unbundled billing inverts it. Prenatal visits become individually reportable encounters. Labor management becomes a day-by-day service with its own documentation requirements. Delivery becomes a procedure code plus whatever add-ons the case actually generated. The number of claim lines per pregnancy goes up substantially, and every one of those lines is a new opportunity for a denial.

THE REAL RISK
Denial volume, not denial rate
A practice with a respectable 6% denial rate on one global claim per pregnancy has a very different workload than the same practice with a 6% denial rate across fifteen claim lines per pregnancy. The rate holds; the absolute number of touches your billing staff must work does not.

The Readiness Checklist

1. Inventory every place a deleted code lives

Deleted global codes are not confined to your billing system. They appear in charge masters, superbills and encounter forms, EHR order preference lists, quick-pick lists, SmartPhrases and templates, patient financial estimates, self-pay price sheets, and any internal report or dashboard that groups volume by CPT. Miss one and it produces a rejected claim on a date when your billing team is already underwater.

Pull the list from the source of truth, a twelve-month claims extract grouped by CPT, rather than from memory. Anything in that extract that CPT 2027 deletes needs an owner and a replacement mapping.

2. Read your payer contracts before your payers read them to you

Many commercial maternity contracts express reimbursement as a case rate tied to a global code. When the global code no longer exists, the contract language has to be renegotiated or it has to be interpreted. And interpretation without a negotiation tends to favor whichever party wrote the interpretation.

  • Identify each contract whose maternity terms reference a deleted code.
  • Ask, in writing, how the payer intends to price unbundled maternity care effective January 1, 2027.
  • Model your top three payers' expected total per-pregnancy reimbursement under the new code set against your current case rate. If the answer is lower, you have a negotiation, not a coding project.
  • Confirm whether Medicaid managed care plans in your state are following the CPT change on the same timeline.

3. Fix documentation before January, not after

The new labor management codes hinge on distinctions your current notes probably do not make cleanly: straightforward versus complex management, initial versus subsequent day, and the specific interventions that separate them. If your labor notes do not currently support those distinctions, the codes will either be undercoded, leaving revenue on the table, or coded without documentation support, which is worse.

This is the longest-lead item on the list, because it requires changing clinician behavior rather than changing a system configuration. Start with a retrospective audit: pull twenty recent labor courses and ask a coder whether the note, as written, would support a complex labor management claim. The gap you find is your training curriculum.

4. Build the cash buffer

Expect a first-quarter dip. New code sets produce edits that nobody anticipated, payer systems lag their own policies, and clearinghouse rules take weeks to settle. Plan for elevated days in A/R through at least March 2027 and make sure whoever manages your operating cash knows that now rather than in February.

PRACTICAL STEP
Dual-code a sample in December
Take a week of December deliveries and code them twice: once under the 2026 global package as you normally would, once under the 2027 component set. You cannot bill the second version, but the exercise surfaces documentation gaps and per-case revenue deltas while there is still time to act on them.

5. Decide who owns the transition

The most common failure mode is diffusion: coding assumes revenue cycle owns it, revenue cycle assumes the EHR team owns the build, the EHR team assumes clinical leadership owns documentation, and clinical leadership has not been told there is a project. Name one person, give them a weekly checkpoint, and give them authority to pull clinicians into training.

What to Do This Month

If you do nothing else in the next thirty days, do these three things: run the twelve-month CPT extract, send the payer letters, and audit twenty labor notes. Each one takes hours, not weeks, and each one tells you how much of the remaining hundred days you actually need.

The transition is manageable. It is only unmanageable on a two-week runway.
